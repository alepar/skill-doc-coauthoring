# Dossier: Syntactic and Structural Tells (F2)

## Summary

This dossier covers the syntactic and structural habits that make prose read as machine-generated, as distinct from vocabulary. The evidence base mixes peer-reviewed corpus linguistics (Reinhart et al. on Biber features; the markdown-fingerprint em-dash study; the reward-model bias study; the expert-annotator study), vendor documentation (Anthropic system prompts and prompting guides, OpenAI guidance), large practitioner or industry measurements (Graphite, Pangram, slop-score, aiwritingbenchmark, The Economist as relayed by secondary outlets) and practitioner style catalogs (Wikipedia's Signs of AI writing, humanizer, stop-slop, avoid-ai-writing, underslop).

The strongest, most durable structural tells are: negative parallelism ("not X but Y" and its reversed, split and clipped forms), sentence-final participial "-ing" riders that add evaluative significance, noun-heavy informationally dense register (nominalizations), copula avoidance ("serves as", "boasts"), low burstiness (uniform sentence length), and formatting imported into prose contexts (bold-label bullets, headers, fragment bullets, small tables). The em dash is the best-known tell but is now strongly model- and version-dependent: it was a GPT-4o/GPT-4.1 and Claude 4.x signature, has collapsed in GPT-5/6, Gemini 3 and Claude Opus 5.5, and its suppression produces its own tells (semicolon cadence, comma-and-period monotony, under-punctuated long "and" chains). Rule of three, summary closers and essay connectives are weaker or era-specific in 2026 data. Over-correction is a documented failure: humanizer tools replace one fingerprint with another (staccato fragments, one-sentence paragraphs, shrug closers).

The practical upshot for /unclaude is that structural scouting should be density-based and position-aware, should treat each hit as weak alone and strong in clusters, and should carry explicit keep-lists so that the fixer does not strip legitimate contrasts, enumerations, run-in list headings or dashes from a writer who uses them.

## Facet Questions and Scope

## Source Groups and Findings

### 1. Negative parallelism ("not X but Y" and variants)

What it is. Wikipedia's editors describe it as follows: it is common for LLMs to use parallel constructions involving "not", "but", or "however", such as "Not only X but Y" or "It is not just X, it is Y" [1]. <!-- claim: 7baac07326196d02; evidence: 70a15efe7e4e7a93; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> A second form states that the item lacks the first characteristic entirely, often as "It is not X, it is Y" or "no X, no Y, just Z" [1]. <!-- claim: cc4f7077736188c5; evidence: b95e1f41ef8ac2be; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> The same source notes the pattern may also be reversed ("Y rather than X"), a construction particularly common in recent LLM output [1]. <!-- claim: f1a0de42076d31c9; evidence: 036cc42297d7e17e; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> The humanizer catalog extends the forms to not just / not only / not merely X but Y, the reversed X rather than Y, the contrast split across two sentences ("This does not mean X. It means Y."), and a clipped negative tail (", no guessing") [3]. <!-- claim: d6cac990e07bb980; evidence: 9053940835b6524a; source: 2db4f7d2d7de0ea2 -->

Strength and rates (industry measurement). On the slop-score leaderboard, Claude Sonnet 4.5's record shows a not_x_but_y rate of 0.171 per 1,000 characters [14]. <!-- claim: 0babcc4044a4944e; evidence: 0e103c201d94b754; source: 4af73d7b1bfdfe6b --> The human baseline record on the same leaderboard is 0.044 per 1,000 characters, so Sonnet 4.5 sits at roughly four times the human rate [14]. <!-- claim: 37eca213a3501112; evidence: 608cff74f6d9a02d; source: 4af73d7b1bfdfe6b --> Pangram reports that AI uses phrases fitting the not-just-X-but-Y template three times as often as humans [33]. <!-- claim: bdd3fbe5e2ba1a6f; evidence: dfeb2bfbd64ec96a; source: be9d090b89232eff --> In aiwritingbenchmark's 1.2-million-word corpus of 2026 frontier output, "not X but Y" contributes 1.5 marks per 1,000 words, against 6.7 for em dashes and 1.8 for lists of three [32]. <!-- claim: 29a3c4628ea05c61; evidence: 8b6a5e1f79eab8eb; source: d159a7ce67ed2d44 -->

Model-family signatures (industry measurement, Graphite). Each family has its own favourite contrast frame. For Claude Opus 5, "rather than merely" runs at 160x the human rate and "less like a _ and more like" at 105x [29]. <!-- claim: 9742ac488358ccf7; evidence: ae739e7545491b75; source: 56493642657ef5f7 --> For GPT-6 Astra, "the _ is not simply" runs at 576x the human rate [29]. <!-- claim: f7cca70d38306d65; evidence: 23460f948e4c2c96; source: 56493642657ef5f7 --> For Gemini 3.1 Pro, "is not just a _ it is" runs at 153x [29]. <!-- claim: 6959ca435f6cec21; evidence: 17695bc3fce6e0ef; source: 56493642657ef5f7 --> Pangram's analyst told The Atlantic that ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and several open-source models all lean on it, to varying degrees, so it is cross-vendor rather than one lab's house style [42]. <!-- claim: 13209c3bfc2d3e0a; evidence: 941de6f74227d4c5; source: 7f0b10b580ddda7b --> Claude-specific catalogs rank the contrastive binary, mirrored-clause symmetry and the aphoristic ender as Claude's most frequent patterns, often fused into an aphoristic ender built as a binary [40]. <!-- claim: b9f4515706640d74; evidence: 879869cb8d2e809e; source: 541132ed5eca2968 --> Generation drift for Claude: between Opus 5 and Opus 5.5 the not-x-but-y structural count fell from 36.5 to 26.1 per 60k words, a 29% reduction [30]. <!-- claim: ab9225ebb458fc53; evidence: 542051156cb68b99; source: ffb7b5602c519659 -->

Detection cues (synthesis). Regex families: `\bnot (just|only|merely|simply)\b[^.]{0,80}\b(but|it'?s|it is)\b`; `\b(it'?s|this is|that'?s) not\b[^.]{0,60}[,;—–-]\s*(it'?s|it is)\b`; `\brather than (merely|simply|just)?\b`; `\bless like an? \w+ and more like\b`; split form: a sentence matching `\b(does not|doesn'?t|isn'?t|is not) (mean|about)\b` followed by a sentence starting `It('?s| is| means)\b`; `\bno \w+, no \w+, (just|only)\b`; clipped tail `, no \w+ing\.$`. A single hit in 1,000 words is within human range; two or more per 1,000 words, or any hit where the negated half names something nobody claimed, is a finding.

Fix. The humanizer rule is the right fix: keep a contrast only when the negative half corrects a belief the reader actually holds, or when both halves carry information; otherwise state the point directly [64]. <!-- claim: c69c54117843f91e; evidence: bf9b010124c2c451; source: 143979a9b24d1841 --> Over-correction looks like deleting genuine misconception-correcting contrasts; Wikipedia itself notes the shape is common among human writers, especially in misconception and myth-busting listicles, even though it is stereotypically an AI sign [1]. <!-- claim: 709327671d32f0b2; evidence: f1b51c8bbcb95754; source: 216545f88fe97a24 -->

### 2. Rule of three / tricolon

What it is. LLMs overuse the rule of three, from "adjective, adjective, adjective" to "short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase", often to make superficial analyses appear more comprehensive [1]. <!-- claim: 3a9e2ce664c9cac6; evidence: b7e8d32af7952701; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> In the peer-reviewed expert-annotator study, annotators flagged the tendency of LLMs to always list examples in groupings of three [84]. <!-- claim: 80deef4eb83d1551; evidence: a36cf6b4a8cf6301; source: 94bc1872a6410141 --> Model note: GPT-5.6 Sol has few dashes but the highest rate of lists of three among top models, 3.3 per 1,000 words, plus a taste for "not just X" [32]. <!-- claim: 8eb91a50fb1c2e77; evidence: 64e178b1aaef7223; source: d159a7ce67ed2d44 -->

Detection cues (synthesis). Pattern: `\b\w+(ly)?, \w+(ly)?,? and \w+\b` and phrase-level triads at sentence end; strengthen when the three items are near-synonyms, abstract nouns, or adjectives with no distinct referent, and when triads recur in consecutive paragraphs. Corpus anchor: 1.8 per 1,000 words is the 2026 frontier average and 3.3 the high end; treat more than about 2 rhetorical triads per 1,000 words as a density finding.

Fix. Cut to the items that carry distinct information (often one or two). Keep literal enumerations (three actual steps, three named products). The rule of three is a weak signal: see Counterevidence.

### 3. Em-dash density, drift and suppression side-effects

What it is. Wikipedia notes LLM output uses em dashes where humans more often use commas, parentheses or colons, often in a formulaic, pat way that mimics punched-up sales writing [1]. <!-- claim: 83ce9f4d9ee3a01c; evidence: 4f064af5fb9edc61; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> The humanizer catalog frames the tell as density: many editors and journalists use dashes, so one dash is weak alone, but a text full of them is not [64]. <!-- claim: bbbc21f2495347f8; evidence: 6936758a95c208ce; source: 143979a9b24d1841 -->

Thresholds (peer-reviewed). The markdown-fingerprint study gives a human baseline of 3.23 em dashes per 1,000 words (mean), with a range of 0.33 to 17.12 [28]. <!-- claim: c3824fedd82debcc; evidence: 25a10978d669add6; source: 0bbcac4a1b3d1437 --> Its unconstrained per-model rates are GPT-4.1 10.62, Claude Opus 4.6 9.09, Claude Sonnet 4 8.29, GPT-4o 4.12, Gemini 2.5 Pro 3.53 and GPT-5.4 1.43 per 1,000 words [28]. <!-- claim: d4355f8667301e25; evidence: a4b94b2a66856b8e; source: 0bbcac4a1b3d1437 --> Explicitly prohibiting the em dash reduces it further, but GPT-4.1 still retains 3.86 per 1,000 words and DeepSeek V3 retains 1.57 [28]. <!-- claim: 7120b8e22e10d9cb; evidence: dc3e9b4c584fe0a7; source: 0bbcac4a1b3d1437 --> The authors propose the em dash is markdown leaking into prose, the smallest surviving unit of structural orientation learned from markdown-saturated training data [28]. <!-- claim: 5852db6d84ac4e73; evidence: 51a75e823216dfa6; source: 0bbcac4a1b3d1437 --> Practitioner thresholds: one editing agency capped dashes at two per 500 words (4 per 1,000) [126]. <!-- claim: f2dbfe7c3b377313; evidence: 4663471b513b9126; source: 2d868d4de107c27b --> Pangram puts the human average at 5 em dashes per 10,000 words with most model families 7x to 9x above, and Gemini 3 Pro below the human rate [33]. <!-- claim: ea32fcf524919679; evidence: 40d4ea844baf06d9; source: be9d090b89232eff -->

Generation drift. GPT-3.5 did not use em dashes, GPT-4o used about ten times more, and GPT-4.1 was even worse [27]. <!-- claim: f5208afb002f564b; evidence: b2adf3d3ad887b30; source: 59cfed295f355dae --> By mid-2026 The Economist found only Claude used more em dashes than human writers, with ChatGPT using markedly fewer than any other writer in its study [112]. <!-- claim: 054b7d8df1bf28fd; evidence: a9c3d10602f2ed09; source: 2273b8c34e950421 --> Graphite's 2026 measurements show GPT-6 Astra at about one-eighth the human rate, Claude Opus 5 at about the human rate, and Gemini 3.1 Pro having nearly stopped [29]. <!-- claim: 87e6bc581d88bc1f; evidence: f3cce7fd586ff878; source: 56493642657ef5f7 --> Claude Fable 5 in contrast scored 14.6 tell marks per 1,000 words, almost all of it em dashes at 12.2 per 1,000 words, one every 80 words [32]. <!-- claim: a4d80da968f99e2a; evidence: 4039950832f30fa0; source: d159a7ce67ed2d44 --> The newest Claude reverses again: Graphite counts 0.015 em dashes per 1,000 words in Opus 5.5 articles against 2.92 in Opus 5, a decline of about 99% [114]. <!-- claim: c7596711e42cb0d4; evidence: 01265b8d3a2265a4; source: a89550d3cce35496 -->

Suppression side-effects (detail in Counterevidence). GPT-5's overuse of semicolons is probably a brevity bias tied to its lower verbosity [27]. <!-- claim: 862f8c78e0d9fd9d; evidence: 6176e92309a885f0; source: 59cfed295f355dae --> For GPT-6-era text Wikipedia notes much more period and comma usage and shorter sentences held to a strict 8 to 20 word limit [1]. <!-- claim: 2ccc661923d14f2a; evidence: 64a763fdc76d89c8; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> This links em-dash suppression directly to the burstiness and under-punctuation tells below.

Fix. The humanizer rule is to replace each dash with a period, comma, colon or parentheses, or rewrite, and to leave dashes in code blocks, inline code, commands, paths and URLs alone [64]. <!-- claim: 4e54ec1a7726c4ec; evidence: 46d42b74a8e65f5c; source: 143979a9b24d1841 --> Synthesis: do not swap mechanically to one replacement mark (that recreates a cadence); choose the mark from the logical relation between clauses. Detection cue: count `—`, ` – ` and spaced ` -- ` outside code; compute per-1,000-word rate; flag above about 4 per 1,000 words (the agency cap and roughly the human mean) and flag clusters (two dashes in one sentence, or the "it's not X — it's Y" fusion with section 1). For Claude Opus 5.5-era input the dash count will usually be near zero, so the lane should not report absence as a finding.

### 4. Colon reveals and throat-clearing pivots

What it is. A throat-clearing pivot is a phrase that promises a revelation and then delivers an ordinary sentence [116]. <!-- claim: c084b27b8cc39926; evidence: 458122d44378f2c7; source: 84f726da84dcf6fb --> In one small corpus the "here is the thing" pivot appeared in 7 of 27 no-think conversations, across several model families [116]. <!-- claim: 0ca5440b43fdec90; evidence: 20e92dfc82662628; source: 84f726da84dcf6fb --> The humanizer catalog lists the staged run-up family: let's dive in, let's explore, let's break this down, here's what you need to know, without further ado, heads up, quick note, here's the thing, the thing is [3]. <!-- claim: 7e070febfc5ecc8a; evidence: bd9da57720f509b4; source: 2db4f7d2d7de0ea2 --> A Claude-specific variant is meta-signposting, narrating the structure instead of having one, which a Claude-pattern catalog says Claude consistently overuses [40]. <!-- claim: ddb7bf3fc448e65b; evidence: 610c4b1565ed187e; source: 541132ed5eca2968 -->

Detection cue. The delete-the-pre-colon test: if you delete everything before the colon, does the sentence lose something, or did the pre-colon phrase do zero work? [120]. <!-- claim: ad549b3de7bda481; evidence: 25779267148a81cf; source: eff07036c2ba8c9e --> Regex: `^(Here'?s|Here is) (the thing|what|why|where it gets)\b`, `^(The (real|short|honest) (answer|truth|point|catch) is)[:,]`, `^(The result|The kicker|The catch|The twist|Bottom line|In short)\s*:`, `\bLet'?s (dive|explore|break)\b`, sentences of the form "Four caveats belong at the front." that count or announce what follows. Position cue: paragraph-initial or sentence-initial only.

Fix. Delete the run-up and start with the content; keep colons whose left side carries content (a definition, a label in a reference list, an introduced quotation).

### 5. Participial "-ing" riders, nominalizations and dense register (Reinhart)

What it is. Wikipedia: chatbots insert superficial analysis of significance, recognition or impact, often by attaching a present-participle "-ing" phrase at the end of sentences [1]. <!-- claim: 6ef740b7fae3b30d; evidence: 87aa81e0fe458322; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> The rider lexicon includes highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting, symbolizing, contributing to, cultivating, fostering, encompassing and enhancing [1]. <!-- claim: 17a845fbf70a670d; evidence: 2006eff918359a86; source: 216545f88fe97a24 -->

Peer-reviewed evidence. Reinhart et al. found instruction-tuned LLMs used present participial clauses at 2 to 5 times the human rate and nominalizations at 1.5 to 2 times [24]. <!-- claim: 8b418bfd51ff13a2; evidence: 273512d990b66b4c; source: e30a1d7165201ec8 --> For GPT-4o specifically: present participial clauses 5.3x, that-clauses as subject 2.6x, nominalizations 2.1x and phrasal coordination 1.9x the human rate [24]. <!-- claim: 74b38e9b3bdb48af; evidence: af8413401d7348d6; source: e30a1d7165201ec8 --> The authors conclude instruction tuning trains models into an informationally dense, noun-heavy style and limits their ability to mimic other styles, producing genre misalignment [24]. <!-- claim: e7ec2f6dacace081; evidence: d1e6273c947bfefd; source: e30a1d7165201ec8 --> A 2026 replication across genres and models confirms LLM outputs are more information dense and noun-heavy, especially for instruction-tuned chat models [25]. <!-- claim: ea5efdd1f4f230de; evidence: 706ba5fa47638ff0; source: a4ad03f933f401ad -->

Detection cues (synthesis). Sentence-final `, (highlighting|underscoring|emphasizing|ensuring|reflecting|symbolizing|contributing to|cultivating|fostering|encompassing|enhancing|showcasing|solidifying|marking)\b[^.]*\.$`; more broadly `, \w+ing\b[^.]{5,}\.$` where the -ing clause evaluates rather than describes an action. Stacked riders (two participial clauses in one sentence) and riders with "its/their significance/importance/role" are strong. Nominalization cue: high density of `-tion|-ment|-ance|-ence|-ity|-ness` nouns with weak verbs (provide, ensure, enable, facilitate). Flag more than about one evaluative rider per 300 words.

Fix. Convert the rider into a finite clause with a named subject, or delete it if it only asserts significance. Turn nominalizations back into verbs. Over-correction: Reinhart also finds GPT-4o uses the agentless passive at roughly half the human rate (see Counterevidence), so a fixer should not convert every passive to active as part of "de-AI".

### 6. Copula avoidance ("serves as", "boasts")

What it is. Wikipedia's words-to-watch list: serves as, stands as, marks, functions as, operates as, represents, boasts, features, maintains, offers, refers to [1]. <!-- claim: 517fa30c64f9cfc2; evidence: 102a9bf98e509612; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> Empirical support (peer-reviewed, via Wikipedia): one study documented an over 10% decrease in "is" and "are" in academic writing in 2023, with no major changes before that [1]. <!-- claim: bcff3c79e18f271e; evidence: 1f2e5dcbf11843b8; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> The pattern is particularly visible in AI copyedits, which often "improve" text this way [1]. <!-- claim: 2a01d90405335792; evidence: 698cc630084be37b; source: 216545f88fe97a24 -->

Detection cue. `\b(serves|stands|functions|operates|acts) as (a|an|the)\b`, `\b(boasts|features) (a|an|over|more)\b`, `\brepresents a (significant|major|key|pivotal)\b`, `\bmarks a (significant|major|turning|pivotal)\b`. Density cue: low is/are counts relative to sentence count in descriptive passages.

Fix. Use is, are or has. Critical for the fixer: because AI copyedits introduce this pattern, the /unclaude reviewer must not "upgrade" a plain copula while fixing other findings.

### 7. Uniform sentence length (low burstiness) and under-punctuation

What it is. Graphite's 2026 study: sentence lengths vary much less in AI writing than in human writing [29]. <!-- claim: 436f88a428120a54; evidence: 24772054d9b0f7da; source: 56493642657ef5f7 --> A researcher quoted by VentureBeat on Opus 5.5 put it as humans writing some short and some very long sentences while LLMs produce sentences more uniform in length [114]. <!-- claim: 8f35a0497fe39d5f; evidence: 875d37275fb017e9; source: a89550d3cce35496 --> GPTZero's framing (vendor) is that a model writes with a very consistent level of AI-likeness, whereas people vary construction and diction across a document, so a single AI-like sentence is not the signal [88]. <!-- claim: 59a8f561f2869643; evidence: 10cf9c1a1f5d6be0; source: cf2471251fdfd300 -->

Under-punctuation (The Economist, via secondary reports). LLMs use fewer commas and semicolons than humans, and hardly any parentheses [112]. <!-- claim: 71c8959993745197; evidence: 43293eb7d1c4c05a; source: 2273b8c34e950421 --> Fast Company's summary adds that they instead build overly long sentences with "and" as their most overused word [113]. <!-- claim: 33b04e7b26eabdc2; evidence: 22114479e5c4324b; source: 653be2e6dab4ca27 --> Graphite similarly finds human writers use exclamation marks over a hundred times as often as current models and interrupt themselves with parenthetical asides far more often [29]. <!-- claim: a2ee8e6fca73027a; evidence: 1a3ed84ca2f854ac; source: 56493642657ef5f7 --> For Claude specifically, Anthropic's own prompting guide says Fable 5.1 prose is denser than Fable 5, with longer sentences and fewer paragraph breaks [46]. <!-- claim: 8c838cbf47af4b0f; evidence: 9c63680261d4ecfd; source: 557f96ccb3270d91 -->

Detection cues (synthesis). Compute per-paragraph and per-document sentence-length standard deviation and coefficient of variation; flag stretches of 5 or more consecutive sentences all within about plus or minus 5 words of each other, or a document-wide coefficient of variation well below that of the writer's own sample. Flag GPT-6-style runs of 8-20-word sentences with only periods and commas. Separately flag sentences over about 35 words that chain clauses with "and" and contain no internal comma, semicolon or parenthesis. Absence of parentheticals and exclamation marks is a document-level signal only, never a per-sentence finding.

Fix. Vary sentences by varying their content: merge two short related claims into one sentence with a subordinate clause, split an "and"-chain at its logical seam, restore a parenthetical or a semicolon where the logic calls for it. Over-correction: staccato fragments (section 11).

### 8. Summary closers, one-line closers and portable intros

What it is. Older LLMs often added sections titled "Conclusion" and ended paragraphs or sections by summarizing and restating the core idea [1]. <!-- claim: bd3c18440fefbbce; evidence: 6301d43d8b2cad1c; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> The expert-detector guide behind the peer-reviewed annotator study says AI text always ends with a neat conclusion rather than ending naturally, and its conclusions are often overly long and summarize everything already written [115]. <!-- claim: 96ca676c5dc268bd; evidence: a39535a475538f74; source: 2dc80b5318ff3a1d --> The newer form is the one-line closer: a one-sentence paragraph restating the previous one ("That is the real win.", "That distinction matters."), or the same closer after several sections [3]. <!-- claim: 274c4da4d17643dc; evidence: 17916e86d563b767; source: 2db4f7d2d7de0ea2 -->

Detection cues. Paragraph-final or document-final sentences beginning `^(In (summary|conclusion|short|the end)|Overall|Ultimately|So,|Bottom line)\b`; one-sentence paragraphs under about 12 words following a longer paragraph; closers with "matters", "the real", "that's the", "read that again". Portable-intro test: if the first paragraph could be used on a different article with minor changes, cut or rewrite it; if the last paragraph only repeats what the reader already knows, delete it or replace it with one specific closing sentence [77]. <!-- claim: 02e3e16916559248; evidence: 17bc8949e169ab42; source: 7ae715e0a2fb5ce2 -->

Fix. One short sentence can carry emphasis when it carries a new fact; cut a closer that repeats [64]. <!-- claim: e77309f22a02abc2; evidence: 3612a2ab98b3bd3b; source: 143979a9b24d1841 -->

### 9. Bold-label bullets, mechanical bolding and excess formatting in prose contexts

What it is. Wikipedia describes vertical lists where the list marker is followed by an inline boldfaced header separated by a colon from the description [1]. <!-- claim: 847358cde4644fc6; evidence: 985955489eaefcc3; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> A practitioner summary notes bold-titled bullets are a common ChatGPT structure, virtually nonexistent on Wikipedia, and the bolded phrase is often just reworded in the sentence after it [4]. <!-- claim: af01fc533d7be550; evidence: 4b8d917d29a63870; source: 5cce2b8dd13a00d9 --> Mechanical bolding, inherited from readmes, how-tos, sales pitches, slide decks and listicles, emphasizes every instance of a chosen phrase in a key-takeaways fashion [1]. <!-- claim: eb0011da8d21318d; evidence: c5fe95ac28c48af3; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> Some models also produce small, minimally formatted tables that would be better as prose [1]. <!-- claim: 822c1db2f26db050; evidence: 2aa54e739b4b27b5; source: 216545f88fe97a24 -->

Root cause (peer-reviewed). Reward models and LLM judges show 89.5% preference for structured responses and 60.1% for verbose ones [103]. <!-- claim: bab01f0747ebfdde; evidence: ed95e7e5e7df37f0; source: 6c44465befb6a074 -->

Model-family differences. In 2024-era measurements (peer-reviewed, Idiosyncrasies in LLMs) ChatGPT bolded each key point in enumerations and topped responses with markdown headers, while Claude used simple enumeration and bullets [21]. <!-- claim: f56c15953e97936e; evidence: ddbe91edc7826efb; source: 5b0ec3b4902b975f --> Claude showed a high density at zero bold and zero headers, while the other models decorated more often [21]. <!-- claim: 04b7a60b9aa5e083; evidence: a1e0e3c806603122; source: 5b0ec3b4902b975f --> Anthropic's guide for Fable 5.1 says earlier models overused bullets and bold in chat but Fable 5.1 leans the other way, using bold less and reaching less for headers, lists or quotation marks [46]. <!-- claim: d6474ab9d8208d85; evidence: 6c3a2312b7cda280; source: 557f96ccb3270d91 --> Anthropic's Opus 5.5 system prompt sets the norm: lists and formatting only when asked or when the content is multifaceted, with bullets of at least 1 to 2 sentences [52]. <!-- claim: 1669e9fab350da55; evidence: 4491995768631720; source: 3e045f52520583bf --> It also gives the fix shape: inside prose, lists read as "some things include: x, y, and z" without bullets or newlines [52]. <!-- claim: cb93560e36f4c98c; evidence: 0379b4baf7371d5a; source: 3e045f52520583bf --> Register matters: in friendly, personal or emotional chats Claude is told to use no formatting at all because formatting lends a formal tone [51]. <!-- claim: d421cd7edfcb76c5; evidence: 711577d6c5cd7788; source: b08f5506b2c43068 -->

Detection cues (synthesis). `^\s*([-*+]|\d+[.)])\s+\*\*[^*]+\*\*\s*[:—–-]` (bold-label bullet); compare the bold label with the first 6 words after the colon and flag when they share the head noun (redundant label); bullets under about 8 words in a document or explanation (fragment bullets); more than one bolded span per paragraph in running prose; a table with 2 or 3 rows or 2 columns; any list or heading in a personal or emotional message.

Fix. Remove the bold; fold label-and-description bullets into sentences when labels carry no information of their own; turn short fragment bullets into an inline series. Keep-list: run-in headings with a colon are a documented style-guide convention (see Counterevidence), and reference material that will be scanned can keep them.

### 10. Headings: overuse, title case and generic templates

What it is. In section headings, AI chatbots strongly tend to capitalize all main words [1]. <!-- claim: 35aec90e3a6add99; evidence: 5d060ad0a6238c00; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> The expert-detector guide lists generic AI header templates such as "Understanding The Topic", "How Topic Works", "Advantages and Challenges" and "Pros and Cons of Topic" [115]. <!-- claim: e9853473388b3e8d; evidence: cc11483655f42965; source: 2dc80b5318ff3a1d --> Plain-language guidance supplies an over-structuring test: with rare exceptions, headings should be shorter than the content that follows them [122]. <!-- claim: 6cbcaa31da1853f0; evidence: 57227a38802481f2; source: f33160b06bab57d6 -->

Detection cues. Title-case heading in a document whose house style or other headings are sentence case; heading-to-body ratio (a heading over a single short paragraph, or sections under about 80 words in a document under 800 words); template matches `^#+\s*(Understanding|How .* Works|Why .* Matters|Key (Takeaways|Considerations)|Pros and Cons|Advantages and (Challenges|Disadvantages)|Conclusion|Final Thoughts)`; "X and Y" paired headings; headings in a short chat answer. Wikipedia reports that the specific heading "Awards and recognition" is nearly ubiquitous in AI-generated articles [1]. <!-- claim: 9cc0d73b550e0bb7; evidence: b6cb14ba8a8f07f2; source: 216545f88fe97a24 -->

Fix. Match the host document's case convention (sentence case by default); remove headings from short pieces; rename generic headings to say what the section claims.

### 11. Staccato fragments and dramatic fragmentation

What it is. The stop-slop catalog names the "[Noun]. That's it. That's the [thing]." template as performative simplicity [65]. <!-- claim: 7aa67c7c0a6d7d02; evidence: 5626d2cf2fa30777; source: 041055887f823eb7 --> Avoid-ai-writing defines staccato conversion as chopping ordinary sentences into fragments to manufacture rhythm, and says to vary length by varying the sentences, not by breaking them [68]. <!-- claim: dee8a1e0308fadcb; evidence: 1a6951c44bf3c435; source: 10ac1554017e831d --> This is both a generation tell (dramatic fragments as emphasis) and the leading over-correction tell produced by humanizer passes (see Counterevidence).

Detection cues. Verbless sentences (no finite verb) under about 5 words, especially two or more in a row; `\. (That'?s it|Period|Full stop|Simple|Every time|No exceptions)\.`; one-sentence paragraphs stacked in sequence.

Fix. Re-attach fragments to the sentence they modify; keep a single fragment where it carries new information.

### 12. Cross-cutting: structure persists while vocabulary drifts

The humanizer authors' rationale for leading with structural patterns is that word habits change with every model release while structural habits persist [3]. <!-- claim: 4de87e484ebafa23; evidence: 1b210c59c5295114; source: 2db4f7d2d7de0ea2 --> This is consistent with the drift data above: em dash and "delve" collapsed across 2025 to 2026, while contrast frames, participial riders, noun-heavy register and low burstiness are still measured in current models.

## Counterevidence and Disagreements

Em dashes as a tell. The strongest disagreement in F2 concerns the em dash. Freeburg's per-model table shows rates from 0 to over 10 per 1,000 words; Graphite reports GPT-6 and Gemini 3.1 well below the human rate; Opus 5.5 has nearly stopped. A humanizer vendor points out that Mark Twain used 10.13 em dashes per 1,000 words, GPT-4.1 uses 10.62, and Meta's Llama models use zero [5]. <!-- claim: 4cc2dd380df14e61; evidence: edb03d835ba545ed; source: c3e630563d972c51 --> The avoid-ai-writing skill treats em dash frequency above 1 per 1,000 words as writing-quality guidance, not evidence of machine authorship, since usage varies by model generation and vendor [68]. <!-- claim: 8d40ee739e2eb85f; evidence: 816ce65bb3a3dbb6; source: 10ac1554017e831d --> The human baselines also disagree by an order of magnitude: Freeburg's 3.23 per 1,000 words versus Pangram's 5 per 10,000 (0.5 per 1,000). This likely reflects corpus genre (literary and professional prose versus general web text), but it means a single fixed threshold is not defensible; the lane should compare against the writer's own sample when one exists and otherwise use about 4 per 1,000 as a quality ceiling, not an authorship signal.

Suppression side-effects. Wikipedia (with a citation-needed flag) reports that dash suppression made LLMs avoid alternate punctuation, so commas and periods now dominate and that monotony has become its own sign [1]. <!-- claim: 7805c859d58e6134; evidence: 0f24e7f94d81fee3; source: 216545f88fe97a24 --> A practitioner warns that swapping em dashes for semicolons as a reflex creates a repeated semicolon cadence that reads as generated as the dashes did [42]. <!-- claim: 6d297ce2e274d66f; evidence: 1c37132241d09e9d; source: 7f0b10b580ddda7b -->

Rule of three. Underslop's logs found the rule of three in only 4 of 12 models, and much of what a simple pattern-match catches is a literal enumeration rather than a rhetorical flourish [119]. <!-- claim: 784fadbfb344df00; evidence: 8467b3a0ef09730b; source: 1ccacfcd3eed4fbf --> This conflicts with Wikipedia and the expert-annotator study, which treat triads as a standard tell, and with aiwritingbenchmark's 1.8 to 3.3 per 1,000 words. Reconciliation (inference): triads are model-specific (strong for GPT-5.6) and only meaningful when the items are rhetorical rather than enumerative.

Essay connectives and summary closers. Underslop checked all 12 models in its corpus for "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally," and "In conclusion" as connective stacking and found zero occurrences [117]. <!-- claim: ca3524a2f7719784; evidence: 184f7607086f5618; source: 16855c7a068bda86 --> Wikipedia also files section summaries under historical indicators. The closer tell has migrated from explicit "In conclusion" to the one-line restating closer and the aphoristic ender.

Sentence-length uniformity. ScriptGrain's 2.1-million-abstract study found average sentence length was 24.46 words in 2020-2022 and 24.49 in 2026, concluding the change is in vocabulary, not rhythm [19]. <!-- claim: d577f6a47f649c8c; evidence: 0c21d7268ac5f352; source: 1b8eed8dc8f57f78 --> This measures the mean, not the variance, so it does not contradict the burstiness finding, but it does show sentence length alone is not a tell in formal academic genres.

Colons. A defense of the colon argues it is a cataphoric signal that points forward, promising that what follows will complete what came before [120]. <!-- claim: 5ee3e733eefcdac2; evidence: 788c40415c6416f4; source: eff07036c2ba8c9e --> Only the degenerate signpost form ("here's the thing:") is a tell.

Bold-label bullets. Google's developer style guide prescribes description lists with run-in headings and says to end the run-in heading with a period or a colon, consistently within the list [96]. <!-- claim: e8075deee718bb85; evidence: e390aea6b2cf309b; source: 48486baf13318ddb --> In technical documentation the format is legitimate; it is a tell in essays, chat replies and narrative prose.

Passive voice. Reinhart et al. find GPT-4o uses the agentless passive at roughly half the human rate [24]. <!-- claim: 796eb15a84d8d1d6; evidence: 5ed1f7cb3760ccab; source: e30a1d7165201ec8 --> A "make it active" rule therefore pushes text toward the model's own register.

Over-correction by humanizers. A stress test of the blader humanizer found it replaced one fingerprint with another, producing fragments, shrug closers, and one-sentence paragraphs stacked like index cards [76]. <!-- claim: dbe7f5d262eb5bb6; evidence: 5476f6d1c7a5eb15; source: 3c875bce4cfa4dff -->

Model-specific resistance. A practitioner reports that an anti-"X/Y" prompt works for Claude, GPT, DeepSeek and others, but Gemini is one of the few that still produces it [124]. <!-- claim: ebb2ae4cb6cf66fd; evidence: 74e95335fe79e06e; source: 17c66d9ac538f880 -->

## Methodological Limits and Open Gaps

- No evidence row in the ledger addresses horizontal rules (`---` between sections) as a tell; any inclusion in a lane is unsupported inference from the general over-formatting evidence.
- Burstiness lacks a published numeric threshold (standard deviation or coefficient of variation) for human versus model text; the cues above are proposed, not measured.
- Several key 2026 measurements (Graphite, aiwritingbenchmark, Pangram, slop-score, Signalbox) are vendor or industry reports without peer review, and they use different units (per 1,000 words, per 1,000 characters, per 60k words, multiples of human rate), so cross-source thresholds are approximate.
- The Economist's under-punctuation finding is cited here through secondary outlets (Daring Fireball, Fast Company), not the original article.
- Model-name labels in 2026 sources (Opus 5, Opus 5.5, Fable 5, Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.1 Pro) are vendor-specific and the generational drift is fast; any per-model guidance will age within months.
- Peer-reviewed Biber-feature results (Reinhart) were measured on GPT-4o and Llama 3; whether current Claude shows the same participial and nominalization multiples is not directly measured in the ledger.
- Title-case evidence is a single practitioner source (Wikipedia) without rates.

## Proposed lane material

Lane key `contrast` - Contrast frames and reveals (per passage).
Hunt: not X but Y; not just/only/merely X but Y; it's not X, it's Y; X rather than (merely) Y; less like X and more like Y; split two-sentence form (This doesn't mean X. It means Y.); "no X, no Y, just Z"; clipped negative tails; throat-clearing pivots and colon reveals (here's the thing, the real answer is:, let's dive in); meta-signposting that announces structure. Fix: state the positive claim directly; delete the run-up and start with the content. Keep: contrasts correcting a belief the reader actually holds or where both halves carry information; colons with contentful left side. Per-model: Claude favours "rather than merely" and "less like / more like" and the aphoristic binary ender; GPT-6 "the X is not simply"; Gemini "is not just a X, it is" and resists suppression.

Lane key `syntax` - Dense register and sentence mechanics (per passage).
Hunt: sentence-final evaluative -ing riders (highlighting, underscoring, ensuring, reflecting, fostering, showcasing); stacked participial clauses; nominalization clusters with weak verbs; copula avoidance (serves as, stands as, boasts, features, represents a, marks a); rhetorical triads of near-synonyms; long "and"-chained sentences without internal punctuation. Fix: finite verb with a named subject; is/are/has; cut triads to the informative items; split and-chains at the logical seam. Keep: literal three-item enumerations; agentless passives where the agent is unknown or irrelevant; is/are must never be upgraded to "serves as" during any fix.

Lane key `rhythm` - Punctuation and rhythm (whole document).
Hunt: em-dash rate per 1,000 words outside code (flag above about 4, or above the writer's sample rate; flag "it's not X — it's Y" fusions and two dashes per sentence); semicolon cadence; comma-and-period monotony and runs of 5+ sentences of similar length (GPT-6 8-20 word band); absence of any parenthetical, question or varied punctuation over long stretches; staccato fragments and "That's it." templates; stacked one-sentence paragraphs. Fix: choose punctuation from the logical relation between clauses, never a single substitute mark; vary sentences by changing their content, never by chopping. Keep: dashes in code, paths, URLs; writer's own dash habit when a sample shows it. Per-model: Claude Fable 5 and Opus 4.x are dash-heavy; Opus 5.5, GPT-5/6 and Gemini 3.x are dash-light, so absence is not a finding; Fable 5.1 runs long sentences with few paragraph breaks.

Lane key `shape` - Document shape and formatting (whole document).
Hunt: bold-label bullets with redundant labels; mechanical bolding; fragment bullets under 1 sentence in documents and explanations; small 2-3 row tables; any list, heading or bold in personal or emotional messages; title-case headings against a sentence-case context; generic headings (Understanding X, Key Takeaways, Pros and Cons, Conclusion); headings longer than or nearly as long as their content; summary closers (In summary, Overall, Ultimately) and one-line restating closers; portable intros; the intro-three-sections-recap essay frame; horizontal rules in short pieces (unsupported inference). Fix: prose with inline series; remove bold; sentence-case headings or remove them; cut restating closers or replace with one specific final fact. Keep: run-in headings in reference or technical docs; lists for genuinely multifaceted or sequential content; headings in long documents meant for scanning. Per-model: GPT and Gemini over-format more than Claude; recent Claude under-formats.

Cross-lane guidance for the reviewer: treat single hits as weak and clusters as strong; never introduce staccato fragments, semicolon cadence, or "serves as" while fixing; prefer the writer's own sample rates over fixed thresholds.
